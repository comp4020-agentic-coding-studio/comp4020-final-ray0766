# Little Worlds PROCESS

*AI-assisted account, revised following the author's review. Personal statements draw on the author's recorded decisions and responses; technical claims link to repository evidence.*

## Choosing a direction

After reading the Final requirements, I asked the agents for project ideas, but the first suggestions seemed too simple. I asked them to research comparable competitions and award-winning work, then examined the examples myself. [Messenger](https://messenger.abeto.co/) interested me most: I visited its website, played it and looked at its community. It gave me a concrete reference for a small explorable 3D world. References helped when words alone produced a style far from what I imagined, but choosing what would make my project different remained my responsibility.

On 4 October, I agreed to begin with a courier prototype based on that core experience. After the agent reported a playable local version, I said it was still far from the reference. Only later did I ask for each player to have a planet to build on, with visitors unable to alter it. Building modules and spaceship travel followed on 5 October. I wanted more than exploration alone. Photography also grew from my wish for players to share their discoveries and buildings; the complete community publishing system remains an intention.

## Organising a second opinion

I deliberately arranged collaboration between GPT and Claude as a form of peer review. One could propose requirements or an approach, while the other checked a limited part of it. I wanted another perspective on quality and less material accumulating in a single conversation. For the overall planet style, GPT suggested several directions; Claude carried out limited validation before I settled on the final direction. I did not measure a quality or token-saving improvement. The useful feature of this arrangement was having something more concrete to judge than one model's description.

Codex worked in the main repository while Claude supplied reusable modules from a separate workshop. I asked for a convincing industrial harbour and building resources of consistent quality, but attractive demonstrations still needed editing, saving and ownership integration. For Crit 8, I prioritised a working release rather than importing everything. The integration boundary is recorded in [103a7ff](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/103a7ff), including the division of work in ADR 0006. Review could also expose unclear requirements. On 12 October, discussion of a teleport report revealed inconsistent explanations of destinations and older homes without stations. At that stage, I simplified the rule to travel between the main planet and the player's own home, instead of adding exceptions ([d725b96](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/d725b96)).

## Connecting implementation and checks

The project used TypeScript, Three.js and Vite, with a native DOM interface and a Node 24 HTTP server backed by built-in SQLite. The recorded rationale was that Three.js allowed custom spherical movement and cameras without a full game engine. Native DOM avoided another UI framework but left focus, dialogs and input coordination to the project. Built-in SQLite avoided native-addon compilation and provided transactions and constraints for ownership and saving; it also made Node 24 a deployment requirement. These were implementation tradeoffs supporting the experience I directed, documented in ADR 0001 ([c02a639](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/c02a639e8dbcba3c06aa0faa812560b55442ba64)).

The one-player-one-planet decision shows how an experience goal became a technical rule. I specified that visitors could look but not edit; the agents proposed and implemented the supporting mechanisms. Commit [733c543](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/733c543) records the rule in CLAUDE.md alongside server-side ownership checks and saved-world protection. Its planet API tests cover competing claims, forged identities, visitor writes and reopening the database. These checks connect the instruction to behaviour the system can reject or preserve. They do not mean I personally designed the database or proved every possible interaction safe.

The technical record also shows why the checks themselves need scrutiny. In [76e95f0](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/76e95f0), adding a tower made a physics test select the wrong body through bodies[0]. The repair selected a stable identifier without weakening the assertions or changing the physics implementation; the test still failed when a real fault was injected. In [cec70b5](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/cec70b5), a build removed a script identifier and broke the loading retry entry point. The fix added Chinese and English regression checks against built output, rather than relying only on source inspection. Both examples make a completion report more meaningful by showing what was actually checked.

## Returning to play and changing course

In my [Crit 9 account](reflections/crit-9.md), walking near a wall could swing the camera towards the sky. I asked for several changes, but some felt worse when I played. I chose to return to the earlier camera, keep its indoor switch for the time being, and concentrate on the upward movement near walls. I then asked the agent to finish the necessary technical checks and hand the work back. I needed to judge the view and controls myself, instead of allowing another cycle of changes to substitute for that decision.

Flight loading gave me another tradeoff to make. Repeated loading bars interrupted travel, so I asked for more to load at the beginning, even if that meant waiting longer before flying. The implementation record describes an initial prepared corridor, background loading and a limited cache of detailed worlds. On one [controlled local Chrome route](docs/evidence/final-hd/historical-flight-loading.json), blocking waits during flight fell from 18 to zero, while initial readiness increased from 5.85 to 7.70 seconds. This was an intermediate build, before automatic travel. That evidence matched the compromise I requested, but it did not guarantee the same result on other devices. The important target was fewer interruptions during play, not the shortest initial load at any cost.

References did not make a decision permanent. I had also used Palworld to describe mouse-following camera behaviour and extended that request to the ship. After more play, however, I repeatedly rejected flight controls that were difficult to use. By the 25 October local stage, I had chosen fully automatic flight and removed the visible flight routes. This reduced the control I had initially wanted in exchange for easier travel. These latest changes are recorded in [9fe0aa4](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/9fe0aa4b8989518a79ff15e701f7e4ef63dd5329), so the older camera requests describe intermediate decisions, not the final integrated arrangement.

## Knowing what remains to be accepted

In this exploration game, I found that AI-led testing could consume many tokens while missing unusual player actions. I did not measure that cost. My own play exposed places where the character became trapped, which is why I want repeatable technical checks alongside people trying less obvious routes. For experience-related repairs, a regression test should be followed by play; this does not require manual approval of every code edit. Crit 10's smoother loading and classmates playing normally were useful observations, not comprehensive acceptance. The integrated implementation and technical close-out are recorded in [865ea0f](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-ray0766/commit/865ea0fe1462d67c612a1308e5cc852ebd7d34df). Deployment has its own [verification record](docs/releases/final-2026-10.md); I have not completed all final human acceptance checks.
