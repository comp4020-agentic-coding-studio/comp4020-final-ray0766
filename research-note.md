# Choosing the goal and judging the result

Agentic development needs people to decide both what is worth building and what would count as a satisfactory result. My argument is that these responsibilities belong together. A poorly chosen goal can produce generic software even when the implementation works, while an appealing idea can still lead to an uncomfortable application. Agents can help with research, implementation and verification, but their progress needs to be judged against intentions that developers have considered themselves. Little Worlds informs this position as a personal project, not a controlled study of AI capability.

SWE-bench provides a useful starting point. Jimenez et al. (2024) evaluate patches for real GitHub issues using repository tests. This makes a convincing explanation insufficient: a proposed change must pass executable checks. Those checks are valuable because they make some claims about correctness testable. However, resolving a specified issue is different from deciding which experience to create. The benchmark does not establish whether a new world is distinctive or whether its camera feels comfortable. These are limits on what its results demonstrate, rather than reasons to dismiss automated evaluation.

SWE-agent examines how the working environment affects performance. Yang et al. (2024) design an agent-computer interface for navigating repositories, editing and running code. Compared with SWE-bench's emphasis on evaluating patches, its benchmark results show how interface design can affect issue-solving performance. Anthropic's guidance similarly recommends giving Claude Code ways to verify its work. The paper offers benchmark evidence; the guidance offers practical advice, not a controlled finding that the workflow suits every project. Neither removes the need to ask whether the task and its checks represent what the developer actually wants.

In Little Worlds, that question began before coding. I rejected the agents' initial ideas as too simple and asked them to find existing creative work. After exploring [Messenger](https://messenger.abeto.co/), I used its small 3D planet as a reference, then chose to add building and visits between players' worlds. References also helped me communicate style when words alone produced results far from what I imagined. Later, I referred to Palworld when describing camera behaviour. These examples suggest a role for references as a shared basis for discussion, not as instructions to copy everything. My experience of repetitive AI suggestions supports my own decision to stay involved in creative choices; it does not establish that AI is generally incapable of originality.

The same responsibility continues during testing. I found that letting AI test the exploration game on its own consumed many tokens and still missed unusual player actions. During my own play, I found places where the character became trapped. This is an account of my experience, without a measured token-cost comparison. It explains why I want agents to run basic, repeatable checks and people to explore awkward routes. A reported failure can then become a specific repair and regression test. For changes affecting movement or comfort, playing again remains necessary before accepting the experience.

A reasonable objection is that human involvement costs time and may reduce the benefit of delegation. That cost needs evaluation; Becker et al. (2025) do not isolate it. Instead, their randomised study challenges the broader assumption that AI assistance saves time: 16 experienced developers completed 246 tasks in familiar open-source repositories, taking 19% longer when early-2025 AI tools were allowed than when they were disallowed. This result concerns task completion time in that setting, not the separate cost of human review or my token expenditure. Benchmark patch success, real-task completion time and subjective experience answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable evidence.

My response is to concentrate human attention where it changes the decision. For low-risk changes to stable rules, credible automated checks can provide acceptance evidence without reviewing every edit. Comfort, consequential failures and goals without reliable checks justify people defining and revisiting the acceptance criteria. References make those criteria discussable; direct play can expose unexpected actions. People can also judge incorrectly, so their reports should become reproducible checks where possible. This is a proposed allocation of responsibility, not an established productivity advantage. Delegation is useful when it helps develop an intention and test it, while people remain responsible for choosing that intention and recognising what the available evidence has not yet established.
