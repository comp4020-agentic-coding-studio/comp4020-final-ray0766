# Acceptance criteria must remain open to revision

My central claim is that agentic development should automate implementation against explicit criteria while keeping those criteria open to revision through use. Developers contribute more than an initial idea and a final approval: they decide when a technically successful result answers the wrong question. Little Worlds led me to this position because several improvements required changing the intended interaction, rather than asking an agent to implement the same instruction more accurately. This is a situated argument from one project, not evidence that this workflow universally improves productivity.

I initially used Messenger as a reference for a compact explorable world, then asked for personal planets with ownership boundaries. Here, acceptance could become relatively stable: a visitor should see another person's construction but be unable to overwrite it. Server-side permission and persistence checks turn that intention into repeatable evidence. Once the rule is clear, requiring me to inspect every implementation step would add little. My responsibility is to choose the boundary and question its coverage; the agent can repeatedly check its enforcement.

Movement exposed a different kind of uncertainty. My own play found trapped characters and flight controls that remained difficult after successive changes. Removing jumping and choosing automatic travel changed what the product should do. A test proving that manual controls followed their specification would not have resolved my dissatisfaction. I therefore distinguish implementation uncertainty, where the target is credible but the patch may fail, from acceptance uncertainty, where the target itself needs examination. The distinction is practical rather than absolute: a reported failure can reveal both, and human judgement can also be mistaken.

The research helps explain why one measure cannot settle both uncertainties. SWE-bench evaluates issue patches through repository tests (Jimenez et al., 2024); SWE-agent studies how a purpose-built interface improves agents' performance in that setting (Yang et al., 2024). These findings support executable feedback when an issue and its tests provide a useful target. They do not show that a developer has selected a worthwhile interaction. Their shared evaluation setting makes the interface result relevant to patch production, while limiting an inference about product acceptance. Anthropic's verification guidance offers a compatible practical recommendation, rather than independent experimental confirmation of broader benefits.

Becker et al. (2025) instead measure developers completing contributions in familiar repositories. Their randomised study of 16 experienced developers and 246 tasks estimates a 19% increase in completion time when early-2025 AI tools were allowed. This need not contradict improved benchmark resolution: a successful generated patch and a finished contribution have different evaluation boundaries. Repository familiarity, implicit context and reviewing unreliable output are plausible contributors discussed by the authors, not isolated causal explanations established by their experiment. Neither study establishes the best supervision policy for an unfamiliar creative project. Together they support asking which outcome matters and which costs an evaluation includes before transferring its result into practice.

In Little Worlds, this means retaining both stable rules and revisable experience goals. I directed three independent agents to explore concurrently, preserve their construction and report failures. Their findings became specific repairs; later returns confirmed the retained worlds after more than 34 hours. Those checks support construction and persistence claims for the tested identities. They cannot establish that every route is comfortable. Keeping the failed route alongside its repair matters because it exposes how acceptance changed, instead of letting the newest green result erase the reason for intervention.

The strongest objection is that revisiting criteria can legitimise endless scope changes and expensive personal review. My response is to require a concrete mismatch before reopening a settled decision. Automate stable, low-risk invariants; concentrate direct play on unclear interactions, consequential failures and claims lacking credible checks. Record what prompted a changed criterion and turn reproducible findings into regressions. People must also accept evidence against their preferences rather than continually moving the target. This is a proposed discipline, not a measured efficiency guarantee. Its aim is accountable delegation: agents make implementation repeatable, while developers remain answerable for the goals, revisions and limits that make those repetitions worth performing.
