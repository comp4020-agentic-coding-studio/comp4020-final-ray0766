# Choosing the goal and judging the result

Agentic development needs people to decide what is worth building and what evidence would justify accepting the result. My argument is that these responsibilities belong together, but human involvement should be proportionate to uncertainty and consequence. Agents can implement and verify well-specified behaviour; developers must still question whether that behaviour represents a worthwhile goal. This is not a claim that more supervision always improves productivity. Little Worlds informs the argument as a personal project, while the research below supplies different, limited kinds of evidence about agentic work.

The first distinction is between a verified patch and an accepted product. Jimenez et al. (2024) construct SWE-bench from real repository issues and corresponding changes. Its evaluation requires relevant failing tests to pass while previously passing tests remain successful. This gives a concrete basis for rejecting plausible but ineffective patches. Yang et al. (2024) use that evaluation setting to examine how agents navigate, edit and execute code. Both studies make executable feedback central, but they answer different questions: SWE-bench specifies a measure of issue resolution; SWE-agent investigates how the agent's working interface affects performance on that measure. Neither directly evaluates whether users want a feature or find its controls comfortable.

That distinction matters when choosing a development workflow. SWE-agent's ablation study reports a 10.7-percentage-point advantage over a shell-only baseline on SWE-bench Lite. This supports investing in the tools and feedback around an agent, rather than treating the model alone as the source of capability. Anthropic's practical guidance likewise recommends runnable verification and separating exploration from implementation. The paper supplies benchmark evidence, whereas the guidance offers a workflow recommendation. Their agreement does not prove that any test suite is a sufficient acceptance criterion. If a mistaken requirement shapes both the patch and its test, iteration can make the implementation more consistent without correcting the requirement.

Becker et al. (2025) complicate the inference from benchmark capability to everyday benefit. In their randomised study, 16 experienced developers worked on 246 tasks in familiar open-source repositories. Allowing early-2025 AI tools increased estimated completion time by 19%, although participants believed the tools had helped. This does not contradict SWE-agent's interface result: the studies examine different tools, tasks and outcomes. A benchmark records patch resolution, while this trial measures developers completing contributions. Nor does the trial isolate the cost of human review or establish that AI always slows development. Its narrower lesson is that perceived assistance and benchmark success cannot substitute for measuring the outcome a workflow claims to improve.

My project illustrates why choosing that outcome matters. I rejected initial ideas as too simple, explored [Messenger](https://messenger.abeto.co/), then directed Little Worlds towards building and visiting personal planets. A reference helped make the intended experience discussable. Later, my own play exposed trapped characters that agent-led checks had missed. These experiences support my decision to judge creative direction and movement directly; they do not establish a general failure of AI creativity or quantify testing costs. The useful connection to the literature is that acceptance evidence must match the claim: ownership tests support ownership rules, while comfort requires observing the relevant interaction.

The strongest objection is that human judgement is also fallible and expensive. Requiring personal review of every change can slow a reliable workflow and introduce inconsistent preferences. Becker's result does not resolve this objection, since it does not compare different supervision policies. I therefore reject a blanket requirement for more human intervention. Where a low-risk task has stable requirements and credible automated checks, additional manual approval may contribute little. Where the intended experience is unclear, a failure is consequential, or no reliable check exists, people have stronger reasons to define and revisit acceptance criteria.

This conditional approach changes what delegation should optimise. Use agents to automate stable rules and reproduce reported failures; use references and direct play to expose assumptions that those checks cannot settle. Turn human findings into repeatable checks where possible, and revise human expectations when evidence challenges them. The allocation should remain open to evaluation rather than being defended as inherently faster. Good agentic practice means choosing a goal deliberately, matching evidence to its acceptance claims, and retaining responsibility for the uncertainty that remains.
